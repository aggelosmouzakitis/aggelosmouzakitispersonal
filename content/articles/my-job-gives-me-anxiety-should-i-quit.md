## PART 2 — ARTICLE

# My Job Gives Me Anxiety. Should I Quit?

How to assess what leaving could resolve, what might remain and which changes are worth testing.

**Anxiety is evidence that something needs attention.** By itself, it does not establish that quitting is the right solution. Leaving may be reasonable when work is harming your health and there is little prospect of meaningful change. In other situations, a different workload, support or treatment could make staying possible.

You might draft a resignation email after another sleepless night, then close it because the mortgage is due. You need to understand what is happening, what could realistically change and what each option would cost.

## First work out what is actually causing the anxiety

Start with specific episodes. What happened before the dread increased? Which tasks or people are involved? Compare what the job requires with what you add because leaving something unchecked feels unbearable. Consider five areas:

- **Workload and role design:** Two vacancies remain unfilled, every project is urgent and responsibility comes without authority to change deadlines.
- **Manager and culture:** Expectations change after delivery, questions attract ridicule or ordinary disagreements threaten your standing.
- **Job insecurity:** Redundancy discussions or a short contract create a genuine threat to income. Repeatedly reading Slack for clues may add to it.
- **Skill and responsibility mismatch:** A promotion brings unfamiliar work without training, or management duties leave little room for work you actually want to do.
- **Patterns that may follow you elsewhere:** You keep polishing completed work, accept every request or experience a routine correction as evidence that you are about to be dismissed.

[Research on work-related mental health risks](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28108676/) associates high demands, low control and poor support with common mental health problems. It cannot establish the cause of one person's anxiety.

The [guide to work anxiety](/work-anxiety/) explains how workplace conditions and existing anxiety can interact. You do not need to assign all responsibility to one side before taking action.

## Signs the job itself may be the problem

Look for conditions you can describe beyond how distressed you feel. Examples include:

- Colleagues also cannot meet expectations within reasonable hours, and vacancies are repeatedly left unfilled.
- Your manager shouts, humiliates people publicly or threatens retaliation for raising concerns.
- You are expected to conceal mistakes, mislead customers or work against important professional values.
- Attempts to clarify priorities produce more demands without anything being removed.
- Your sleep, health or ability to function keeps deteriorating despite reasonable efforts to recover and obtain support.

The [WHO identifies understaffing, bullying and unclear roles among workplace mental health risks](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-at-work). A wellbeing seminar does little to establish whether those conditions have changed.

Other people appearing fine does not settle the question. They may have different duties, more protection or difficulties you cannot see. If work is [affecting your relationship](/work-affecting-relationship/), include that cost rather than judging the situation solely by your performance review.

## Signs changing jobs might not solve everything

Perhaps the same fear has appeared under several managers, including supportive ones. You reread a routine email repeatedly, ask a colleague whether you sounded competent, feel briefly reassured and then need to ask again. A lighter week barely changes the checking.

This may suggest that [anxiety and overthinking](/anxiety-overthinking/) need attention alongside the job decision. The Centre for Clinical Interventions explains how [repeated reassurance can relieve fear briefly while reinforcing anxiety over time](https://www.cci.health.wa.gov.au/News/New-Resources-to-Help-Manage-Reassurance-Seeking). Necessary quality checks and requests for clear instructions serve a different purpose.

Repeating difficulties across roles does not prove that you are the problem. You may have repeatedly entered understaffed workplaces, or still be recovering from an earlier experience. Feeling better on holiday is also a clue with several possible explanations, including rest and temporary distance from a feared situation.

Leaving can improve the conditions while some anxiety remains. You can change jobs and work on recurring patterns at the same time.

## Are you actually burned out?

You may worry about tomorrow while feeling too exhausted to care about the work itself. Tasks that once felt manageable take longer, and you become impatient with people who need you.

The [WHO's occupational framing of burnout](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-05-2019-burn-out-an-occupational-phenomenon-international-classification-of-diseases) includes exhaustion, distance or cynicism towards work, and reduced professional efficacy. Burnout is an occupational phenomenon, rather than a psychiatric diagnosis. Anxiety, depression and physical health problems can overlap with this experience.

A good performance review does not assess your health. The [burnout at work guide](/burnout-at-work/) looks at what recovery requires when you are still delivering. Persistent exhaustion or difficulties functioning warrant assessment, rather than assuming resignation alone will resolve them.

## What to test before resigning

Where it is safe and feasible, test a change that addresses a plausible cause. You are looking for information about whether the role can become workable. You do not have to complete these tests before leaving an abusive or unsafe situation.

| Possible change | What to ask or try | What to observe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reduce scope | Remove a project, change a deadline or secure additional cover. | Does the work actually decrease, or merely move into your evenings? |
| Clarify priorities | Ask your manager which task should wait when demands conflict. | Are priorities clear and respected when new work arrives? |
| Take leave | Use annual leave for rest; discuss sickness absence if you are unwell. | What improves away from work, and what will change on return? |
| Test a boundary | Agree an end to routine evening messages, where duties allow. | Is the agreement respected? What happens to your fear of being unavailable? |
| Stop voluntary overwork | Decline an optional extra or finish routine work at the agreed standard. | Is more effort required by the employer, or driven by your fear of disappointing someone? |
| Explore an internal transfer | Investigate another team or role. | Would it change the manager, duties or conditions that concern you? |

A manager conversation can be concrete: “I can complete A and B by Friday. C needs another person or a later deadline. Which should take priority?” [Acas describes adjustments to tasks, deadlines and responsibilities](https://www.acas.org.uk/reasonable-adjustments/mental-health-adjustments) that may help. If your manager is the source of harm, seek another appropriate route, such as a union representative, occupational health or independent advice.

Agree when to review an implemented change. A fortnight might show whether evening boundaries are respected; longer may be needed to assess recovery. These are examples, not clinical waiting periods. **Worsening health is a reason to review sooner.** A promise without implementation gives you little reason to extend the trial indefinitely.

A [first-person Patient.info discussion](https://patient.info/forums/discuss/anxiety-is-making-me-want-to-leave-my-job-373028) describes relief during leave followed by renewed anxiety, despite supportive colleagues and an unchanged workload. This is an individual account, not scientific evidence. It illustrates why being listened to and having the demands change are separate things to check.

If anxiety means you cannot work or your health is worsening, speak to a GP. [Sickness absence may provide time to recover](https://www.acas.org.uk/managing-work-related-stress/time-off-with-stress); follow the relevant absence policy and check pay and documentation requirements. Leave can create space for assessment without committing you to resignation.

## When quitting is a reasonable conclusion

Leaving becomes more reasonable when the main source of harm is built into the role, changes have failed or are unlikely to happen, and the cost of continuing is substantial. Repeated humiliation, unsafe demands or a serious values conflict may already provide enough information. Seeking advice about an exit can begin before another attempt to negotiate.

A respectable employer can offer a role that no longer fits. You are allowed to leave without proving that it is abusive. Ask what a different role must change: fewer direct reports, predictable hours, clearer authority or work you can support ethically. That gives you criteria for assessing vacancies.

Having recurring anxiety does not oblige you to stay. It does mean that an exit plan may need psychological or medical support as well as another employer. If you remain temporarily, specify what you are staying for and when you will reconsider.

## Don't psychologise the financial reality

A mortgage, dependants or an employment-linked visa can sharply limit your options. Fear about losing income may reflect accurate arithmetic. Healthcare, savings, debt and the availability of suitable work also belong in the decision.

Write down essential monthly costs, accessible savings and any confirmed income during leave or a notice period. Compare finding another role while employed with a period without earnings. Include a slower job search than you hope for, without treating any fixed number of months' savings as universally sufficient.

[A systematic review of unemployment and re-employment](https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/en/publications/mental-health-effects-of-unemployment-and-re-employment-a-systema/) found associations with worsening and improving mental health respectively, but rated the evidence low certainty. These population findings cannot tell you whether leaving your particular job will help. They do challenge the assumption that unemployment is automatically restorative.

Check [resignation implications with Citizens Advice](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/work/resigning/deciding-whether-to-resign/) or an appropriate adviser before giving notice, including effects on pay, benefits or potential claims. Obtain qualified immigration advice if your status depends on employment. Staying while seeking an alternative can be sensible; if staying is becoming unsafe, get help reviewing the options urgently.

## Why some people stay stuck in the decision for months

Sometimes information is missing: you do not know your sick pay, whether a transfer exists or what another employer would offer. Those questions can be investigated.

In other cases, you know the practical options and keep asking someone else whether leaving would be a mistake. Each conversation settles you briefly, then the question returns. You may be seeking a guarantee that no decision can provide.

There can also be grief in leaving. You invested years in a career, wanted this promotion or liked being recognised as successful. [Achievement and self-worth](/achievement-self-worth/) can become closely connected, so a less prestigious role feels personally diminishing even when it suits your life better. Leaving may mean accepting that one hoped-for future is no longer attractive.

Try writing down what would justify staying, what would justify leaving and which unanswered questions could genuinely change your judgement. Put dates beside the questions you can investigate. If every answer creates another hypothetical, the difficulty may now include tolerating uncertainty and possible regret. [Therapy for difficult career decisions](/career-transition-therapy/) can address that part without pretending the financial constraints have disappeared.

## Therapist, career coach or mentor?

A career coach can help compare roles, prepare conversations and organise a job search. A mentor with relevant industry experience can assess whether expectations are realistic and suggest alternatives. Something being common in your industry does not make it harmless.

A therapist can help when the decision repeatedly becomes tangled with panic, shame, fear of disappointing people or an inability to stop checking. [Research on psychological treatment for generalised anxiety](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37271039/) supports work on intolerance of uncertainty. That does not establish that your job decision is an anxiety disorder. A therapist should help you examine your experience and exercise your judgement, rather than decide whether your employer is good or bad.

The distinction between [therapy and coaching](/therapy-vs-coaching/) concerns what kind of help the problem needs. If anxiety is affecting sleep, everyday functioning or your ability to work, [speak to a GP](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/feelings-symptoms-behaviours/feelings-and-symptoms/anxiety-fear-panic/). Assessment can guide treatment and discussions about leave. Employment disputes need appropriate employment advice.

If you cannot keep yourself safe, [seek immediate help](https://www.nhs.uk/nhs-services/mental-health-services/where-to-get-urgent-help-for-mental-health/). In the UK, call 999 or go to A&E. Elsewhere, contact your local emergency service.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will quitting my job cure my anxiety?

Leaving may remove a major source of distress, but it cannot guarantee that anxiety ends. Recovery can take time, and checking, fear of criticism or broader worries may remain. After leaving, assess what improves and what still needs help. Continued anxiety does not, by itself, mean that leaving was a mistake.

### Should I take sick leave before deciding whether to quit?

If you are unwell or struggling to function, discuss this with a GP and follow your workplace's sickness procedure. Sick leave may create time for treatment and practical planning. Check your actual pay entitlement and agree manageable contact where possible. You do not have to settle your entire career decision before seeking medical help.

### How long should I try to fix work anxiety before leaving?

There is no universal deadline. Choose a review date that matches the change being tested, your health and the consequences of waiting. Assess what has actually changed, rather than restarting the clock after each reassuring conversation. If conditions remain harmful or your health deteriorates, seek support and reconsider earlier. Serious harm does not require a trial period.

### What if I need the money and cannot afford to quit?

Start with the options you can access: medical support, changes to duties, an internal move, employment advice or a job search while still employed. Work out the financial consequences of each before giving notice. If they all seem impossible, ask an appropriate adviser to review your situation; assumptions about sick pay, benefits or contractual rights may need checking.
