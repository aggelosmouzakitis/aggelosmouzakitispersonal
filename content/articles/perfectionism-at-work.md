# Perfectionism at Work: When High Standards Start Costing You

Some work needs exceptional care. The difficulty begins when extra checking, preparation or control keeps expanding without a clear improvement in what the work delivers.

The report already answers the question. You spend another hour changing its wording, then reopen it after dinner. Meanwhile, the decision it was supposed to inform is waiting. You know the extra work is becoming disproportionate, but sending it still feels premature.

Perfectionism at work can make stopping difficult even when you understand the requirements. Sometimes you are addressing a genuine quality problem. Sometimes you are trying to prevent criticism or escape the feeling that you have not done enough. Both can happen within the same task.

The useful question is what the additional effort achieves, including what it prevents you from doing elsewhere. A worthwhile change must preserve the standards the job requires while addressing effort that has become difficult to justify.

## High standards and perfectionism are not automatically the same thing

A senior engineer checking a migration plan carefully may be doing exactly what the work needs. Repeatedly changing a routine update after its facts are correct may serve a different purpose. The visible behaviour matters, but so do the consequences of an error and the reason you keep going.

Researchers commonly distinguish *perfectionistic strivings*, involving excessively high standards, from *perfectionistic concerns*, involving self-criticism, doubts and worries about evaluation. These dimensions can coexist. The [2026 review of perfectionism at work](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-020924-064336) also considers the workplace, leadership and team context, beyond perfectionism as an individual trait.

The distinction is more complicated than assigning good and bad labels. A [2025 meta-analysis of work performance](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/joop.70050) found small positive associations between strivings and performance. Both strivings and concerns were associated with longer hours; concerns showed no overall association with better performance. Most studies were cross-sectional, so they cannot establish what caused what.

**High standards need a reason and a stopping point.** Notice whether yours respond to the task, or remain fixed even when further work contributes little. Anxiety about stopping deserves examination, alongside the actual quality requirements.

## What perfectionism looks like at work

You may see yourself as reliable rather than perfectionistic. The pattern becomes easier to recognise through ordinary decisions:

- **Spending two hours on a 20-minute task:** a routine update gets repeatedly revised although its meaning is already clear.
- **Avoiding delegation:** you retain work a capable colleague could do because their different approach feels unacceptable.
- **Rewriting work nobody asked to be rewritten:** a usable document acquires extra sections while more important work waits.
- **Delaying decisions:** you request another analysis without identifying which finding would change the choice.
- **Being defensive around criticism:** you spend the feedback conversation explaining why the problem occurred before checking whether the correction is useful.
- **Avoiding unfamiliar projects:** you stay with work you can already do well because the learning stage feels exposing.
- **Treating minor errors as reputation events:** a harmless wording correction becomes an evening spent imagining what colleagues now think of you.

These examples need context. A colleague may genuinely need training; criticism may be unfair. Assess whether the effort is necessary, whether it changes the result and what happens when you try to stop. The [guide to work anxiety](/work-anxiety/) examines other reasons work can feel threatening.

The effects can reach colleagues too. Rewriting acceptable work or holding decisions open may leave people waiting, uncertain about the standard or reluctant to take ownership.

## How perfectionism causes procrastination

You can spend a busy morning organising references for a proposal while avoiding its first paragraph. The research feels productive, but you are delaying the point at which your thinking becomes visible and open to correction.

For some people, starting means confronting a draft that falls short of what they want to produce. Postponing briefly avoids that discomfort. Later, the deadline makes starting unavoidable, leaving less time to review the work. A rushed outcome can then appear to confirm that even more preparation was needed.

That is one possible pattern, rather than an explanation for every delay. A [meta-analysis of perfectionism and procrastination](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/112533/) found concerns associated with more procrastination, while strivings were associated with less. Wanting to do excellent work does not automatically explain difficulty starting.

In a [Reddit discussion about perfectionism and procrastination](https://www.reddit.com/r/productivity/comments/fcuusc/perfectionism_causing_procrastination/), one contributor described postponing assignments for fear of mistakes, then rushing near the deadline. This is an individual account, not representative evidence; it illustrates how delay can undermine the quality someone wanted to protect.

Ask what information the next hour of preparation will provide. Missing requirements may need clarification. Avoiding a reviewable draft needs a different response: make a limited first version and identify the question you want feedback to answer.

## Perfectionism and imposter syndrome

You finish a difficult project, but decide it only succeeded because you checked everything repeatedly. You then treat that checking as evidence that a genuinely capable person would have needed less effort.

A [2025 meta-analysis](https://ray.yorksj.ac.uk/id/eprint/12307/) found a substantially stronger association between imposter phenomenon and perfectionistic concerns than between imposter phenomenon and strivings. It does not establish a single causal direction or mean that everyone with demanding standards feels fraudulent.

The two patterns can reinforce each other in practice. Fear of appearing inadequate may lead to excessive preparation; success then gets attributed entirely to that preparation, leaving the doubt unchanged. The guide to [imposter syndrome at work](/imposter-syndrome-at-work/) examines this behaviour and distinguishes it from a genuine skill gap.

## Perfectionism and burnout

Another evening of checking reduces the time available to recover. If this repeats, you may keep delivering while feeling progressively more exhausted and resentful. The extra effort can also hide an unreasonable workload from people who only see completed tasks.

A [meta-analysis covering work, education and sport](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26231736/) found stronger positive relationships between burnout and perfectionistic concerns than between burnout and strivings. These associations support taking concerns seriously, without establishing that perfectionism causes every case of burnout.

The [WHO occupational definition of burnout](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-05-2019-burn-out-an-occupational-phenomenon-international-classification-of-diseases) includes exhaustion, detachment or cynicism towards work, and reduced professional efficacy. The guide to [burnout at work](/burnout-at-work/) explains recovery and the conditions that may need changing.

Look at the workload before deciding the problem is yours to solve psychologically. HSE's [Management Standards](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/overview.htm) recognise demands, control and role clarity among important work-design factors. An impossible deadline needs renegotiation. A manageable deadline repeatedly extended by your own revisions may need a change in how you work. Both can require attention.

## Why seniority can make perfectionism worse

You are asked to recommend a direction with incomplete information. Several options are defensible, and the result will depend partly on decisions other people make. Experience helps your judgement, but cannot provide certainty about the outcome.

Some senior roles create more of these situations. If you rely on an indisputably correct answer to feel comfortable, you may respond by requesting detail that cannot settle the uncertainty. A strategic discussion becomes repeated document revision; delegation becomes continuous intervention.

This is a possible response to ambiguity, rather than an inevitable effect of promotion. Junior work can also be ambiguous, and responsibility can justify greater scrutiny. Distinguish information that could change the decision from information you want because deciding still feels uncomfortable.

Your expectations also become other people's working conditions. When acceptable work keeps coming back with newly added requirements, colleagues cannot plan reliably. Agreeing standards matters for the team as well as for your own [ability to switch off](/cant-switch-off-from-work/).

## Why “just lower your standards” is bad advice

You know some mistakes have serious consequences. Advice that ignores those consequences will be difficult to trust. The better question is which aspects of this particular task need which level of care.

Talking about “95%” or “70%” quality can be convenient shorthand, but it does not define what is safe or useful. An internal draft can be unpolished while still needing accurate figures. A final deliverable can require excellent work without needing every stylistic preference satisfied.

| Work situation | What quality requires | A useful stopping rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Safety-critical or regulated work | Applicable procedures, tests and competent review | Required evidence and authorisation are complete |
| An internal draft for discussion | Accurate essentials, clearly marked uncertainties and a reviewable proposal | Reviewers can answer the question the draft is meant to test |
| A routine progress update | Correct facts, relevant risks and a clear next action | The recipient has the information needed to act |

These are examples for agreeing criteria, not permission to override professional requirements. **A task's standard should be explicit enough to check.** If colleagues disagree about readiness, clarify the disagreement before treating either person as perfectionistic.

Quality also includes timeliness. A beautifully revised update arriving after the decision may be less useful than the clear version you had earlier.

## How to reduce the cost without becoming mediocre

Choose one recurring, low-risk situation. The Centre for Clinical Interventions' [perfectionism resources](https://www.cci.health.wa.gov.au/resources/looking-after-yourself/perfectionism) include testing beliefs through changes in behaviour. These workplace applications aim to gather information while preserving necessary checks.

### Define done before beginning

Write down the purpose, required content and review conditions before opening the document. For an update, that might mean progress against the agreed milestone, the current obstacle and who needs to act. When you add another requirement, identify what it changes for the recipient.

### Time-box low-risk work

Give a familiar task a reasonable preparation limit, with its essential checks included. If the limit proves unrealistic, examine whether the task was underestimated, the scope expanded or you repeatedly revisited completed work. Use that information to adjust the plan; turning the time limit into another flawless target defeats its purpose.

### Delegate with agreed standards

Agree the outcome, constraints and review points with someone who has the capacity and competence to do the work. Ask for a correction when an agreed requirement is missed. If the result meets the standard but differs from your preference, notice the urge to rewrite it before acting. Coaching may help with [practical delegation and leadership](/professional-coaching/).

### Seek feedback earlier

Show the relevant reviewer a draft when their input can still change the direction. Explain what is provisional and which question you need answered. This reduces the risk of polishing an approach nobody needs, while keeping unfinished work within an appropriate review context.

### Deliberately tolerate minor imperfection

Start with an optional refinement, such as making an internal layout more elegant after it is already readable. Record what you predict will happen if you omit that refinement, then compare the prediction with the response. A disappointing outcome may reveal a real requirement. Review it rather than insist the experiment must prove you wrong.

### Separate reversible and irreversible decisions

Establish what undoing the decision would actually involve. A change that can be rolled back may still expose data, interrupt a service or consume substantial time. Where consequences are genuinely limited, agree a small trial and review point. Reserve greater scrutiny for consequential commitments rather than requiring equal certainty everywhere.

Track whether these changes reduce delay and unnecessary effort without weakening agreed quality. If [overthinking](/anxiety-overthinking/) simply shifts to a new precaution, examine what you believe that precaution prevents.

## When perfectionism becomes a psychotherapy issue

You understand the task is finished, but stopping brings intense guilt. A correction is useful, yet you feel ashamed long after making it. Work habits may be tied to what mistakes mean about you, particularly when [achievement has become central to self-worth](/achievement-self-worth/).

Therapy becomes worth considering when this repeatedly drives overwork, avoidance or conflict despite clearer expectations and practical changes. It can examine the beliefs involved and help you test different responses. Earlier experiences may matter, but perfectionism alone does not establish a history of critical parenting or trauma.

There is evidence for psychological treatment. A [systematic review of 15 randomised trials](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10135087/) found CBT for perfectionism reduced perfectionism and some associated symptoms. Participants were relatively young on average, and comparisons with other active treatments were limited. It does not prove one approach is best for every professional or guarantee a particular outcome.

A manager or coach may be more appropriate for unclear priorities or delegation skills; the guide to [therapy versus coaching](/therapy-vs-coaching/) explains the distinction. If anxiety is difficult to cope with or affects daily life, [NHS guidance recommends seeking help](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/feelings-symptoms-behaviours/feelings-and-symptoms/anxiety-fear-panic/), including speaking to a GP. Changes at work and personal support can proceed together.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is perfectionism always bad for your work?

Its effects vary. Research distinguishes striving for demanding standards from concerns about mistakes and judgement, and the two can occur together. Evaluate the actual consequences: whether the work meets its purpose, whether extra effort improves it and whether the approach is sustainable. A careful method can remain useful even while repeated revisions, delayed feedback or difficulty recovering need attention. There is no requirement to abandon ambition to investigate those costs.

### Why can't I delegate even when I know I need help?

Clarify whether the obstacle is capacity, competence, an unclear brief or discomfort with another person's approach. Those problems require different responses. If a capable colleague meets the agreed standard and you still feel compelled to take over, examine the outcome you imagine your intervention prevents. Start with suitable work and clear review points. Delegation can involve responsibility and oversight without requiring you to redo every detail yourself.

### How can I tell perfectionistic procrastination from a genuine delay?

Ask what you are waiting for and what would let you proceed. A missing approval or essential input may justify waiting. Repeatedly preparing without producing anything reviewable may indicate difficulty tolerating an imperfect beginning, but overload, fatigue and unclear requirements can also contribute. Look at the sequence around a particular task. If delay persists across situations or seriously affects functioning, an assessment can consider explanations beyond perfectionism.

### Can I work on perfectionism without therapy?

You can begin by clarifying standards and testing a small change in a suitable task. Structured self-help, feedback from a manager or support from a coach may be sufficient for some difficulties. Look for observable changes in behaviour rather than complete comfort with every decision. If the same fear keeps recreating the pattern, or distress affects sleep, relationships or daily life, professional psychological or medical support may help assess what is maintaining it.
