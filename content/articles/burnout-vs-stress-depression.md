# Burnout vs Stress vs Depression: How to Tell the Difference

Stress is a response to demands. Burnout describes a pattern associated with prolonged workplace stress. Depression is a recognised clinical condition that can affect mood, interest and everyday functioning. Their symptoms overlap, and this article cannot diagnose which you are experiencing.

You may still answer messages and meet deadlines, then have nothing available for the evening. Or feel much better on Saturday until thoughts of Monday return. Those details matter, but neither tells the whole story. Understanding what has changed, where it shows up and how long it has lasted is more useful than deciding from one symptom whether you are stressed, burned out or depressed.

## Stress, burnout and depression at a glance

These are broad tendencies. People can experience several at once, and none of the rows is a diagnostic test.

| Feature | Stress | Burnout | Depression |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Typical relationship to cause | Pressure from identifiable demands, sometimes ongoing | Prolonged occupational stress that has not been successfully managed | May have an obvious trigger, including work, or no clear trigger |
| Energy | Tense or overloaded; exhaustion is also possible | Depletion is central | Often reduced; restlessness or agitation can also occur |
| Relationship with work | Work may feel pressured or difficult to manage | Exhaustion, distance or cynicism towards work, and reduced professional efficacy | Can affect work and life beyond it; work may still be a major contributor |
| Does rest help? | Often, especially if demands ease | May help, but rest alone may leave the causes unchanged | May provide relief, but is not a reliable test or sufficient treatment |
| Formal diagnosis? | Stress itself is not a psychiatric diagnosis | WHO classifies it as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition | A recognised clinical condition requiring assessment |

The table draws on [WHO's occupational definition of burnout](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-05-2019-burn-out-an-occupational-phenomenon-international-classification-of-diseases) and [NHS guidance on depression](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/depression-in-adults/symptoms/). **Energy levels and the effect of a holiday cannot settle a diagnosis.**

## What stress tends to feel like

The deadline moves forward, someone leaves your team and the work is redistributed without anything being removed. You start waking earlier, rereading messages and rushing through conversations at home. There is a recognisable pressure, even if you cannot resolve it yourself.

[Stress can affect thinking, behaviour and the body](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/feelings-symptoms-behaviours/feelings-and-symptoms/stress/). It can involve tension, poor concentration, irritability or changes in sleep. Feeling depleted does not automatically move you into a different category.

Stress may ease after a demanding period, but chronic understaffing has no convenient finish date. Ask whether the pressure is temporary, whether you have enough control and whether recovery actually happens between demands. If anticipating the next problem keeps occupying you even when nothing needs doing, the guide to [work anxiety](/work-anxiety/) explores that pattern more closely.

## What burnout tends to feel like

You used to engage with a difficult project. Now you get through its meetings with increasing distance, and the same tasks seem to require much more effort. You may doubt whether your work achieves anything, even while continuing to deliver it.

WHO describes burnout through three dimensions: exhaustion, greater mental distance or cynicism towards the job, and reduced professional efficacy. Its framing concerns work specifically, although effects on sleep and relationships can extend beyond working hours. Exhaustion alone is insufficient to establish burnout.

A useful next question is what has become unsustainable. An impossible workload, little control, unfair treatment and work that conflicts with your values may require different changes. The fuller guide to [burnout at work](/burnout-at-work/) examines those causes and recovery.

Performance can conceal the cost of continuing. In [high-functioning burnout](/high-functioning-burnout/), the deadlines may still be met while sleep, relationships and life outside work deteriorate. Visible output is an incomplete measure of how someone is doing.

## What depression can look like

The meeting ends, but the heaviness remains. A familiar activity feels uninteresting, decisions become difficult, or you keep concluding that you are letting everyone down. Depression can develop gradually, so the change may be easier to notice in what you have stopped doing than in one dramatic moment.

Common features include persistent low mood or loss of interest and pleasure. Changes in sleep, appetite, energy or concentration can accompany feelings of hopelessness, guilt or worthlessness. Anxiety and irritability can also occur. You do not need every symptom, and continuing to work does not exclude depression.

[NICE recommends a comprehensive assessment](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng222/chapter/Recommendations) that considers severity, history, duration, course and the effect on daily functioning. Counting symptoms from a webpage cannot provide that assessment.

**A work-related cause does not exclude depression.** Research combining prospective studies and individual participant data found an [association between high demands with low control and clinical depression](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5471831/). It was observational evidence, with important limitations, rather than proof of the cause in any individual case. Workplace changes can remain relevant alongside treatment.

## Can you have more than one at the same time?

Yes. You can be dealing with continuing pressure, feel exhausted and detached from work, and also have symptoms that need assessment for depression or anxiety. These descriptions do not form three separate boxes into which everyone fits.

A [systematic review and meta-analysis](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00284/full) found associations between burnout, depression and anxiety. Its authors favoured treating them as related but distinct constructs. Other researchers have [questioned how clearly burnout and depression can be separated](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25638755/). Measurement differences and limited evidence about cause and effect complicate the picture.

It is therefore too certain to say that untreated burnout inevitably becomes depression, or that the two are always unrelated. In practice, a useful assessment considers both the working conditions and the wider symptoms. You can seek help with one while the other is being investigated.

## What happens if you feel better when you're away from work?

Relief provides information about context. If your sleep improves when a particular manager is away, or dread returns when you reopen your inbox, work deserves close attention. That pattern still cannot tell you whether the experience is stress, burnout, anxiety or depression.

Consider what “away” actually means. Are you offline with someone covering your responsibilities, or checking messages from a different location? Is the workload reduced, or merely waiting for your return? A holiday with no cover can be a week spent anticipating a larger backlog.

In a [Mumsnet discussion about struggling to enjoy annual leave](https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/_chat/5199959-incapable-of-enjoying-annual-leave-anymore), contributors described both continuing to monitor work and returning to tasks nobody had covered. These are individual accounts, not diagnostic evidence. They illustrate why “I took leave and still felt bad” needs more context.

A good afternoon or an enjoyable trip does not rule out depression. Equally, failure to relax does not prove it. Notice the pattern without setting yourself a holiday recovery deadline. If work remains mentally present throughout leave, [difficulty switching off](/cant-switch-off-from-work/) may be part of what needs addressing.

## What happens if nothing feels good anymore, including outside work?

You finally have time to see a friend, but cannot find the interest to arrange it. Even activities you normally want to do feel flat. Persistent loss of interest or pleasure, particularly alongside low mood or hopelessness, warrants broader assessment rather than assuming the problem is entirely occupational.

This experience is sometimes called *anhedonia*. It can occur in depression, but it is not a diagnosis by itself. Exhaustion and other health difficulties can also affect enjoyment. A GP can explore the whole picture, including possible physical contributors.

Describe the change concretely: what you previously enjoyed, what you now avoid, and whether the feeling persists when work demands ease. If [work is affecting your relationship](/work-affecting-relationship/), include that too. The person assessing you needs to know more than whether you are still managing your job.

## Which kind of help makes sense?

### Address the working conditions

If the team cannot meet its targets within available hours, the workload needs examination. Ask which deadlines can move, which responsibilities can be removed and who provides cover. If speaking to your manager is unsafe or ineffective, another manager, HR or a worker representative may be a more useful route.

The [HSE Management Standards](https://www.hse.gov.uk/stress/standards/overview.htm) cover demands, control, support, relationships, role and change. [WHO also recommends interventions directed at working conditions](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-at-work). Psychological support can accompany these changes. It cannot supply missing staff or make an abusive manager safe.

### Speak to a GP when symptoms persist or functioning changes

The NHS advises seeking help for [depression symptoms lasting most of the day, every day, for more than two weeks](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/depression-in-adults/diagnosis/). Seek help sooner if you are struggling significantly, worried or deteriorating. The two-week marker is not an instruction to wait through severe symptoms.

A GP can assess mental health and consider physical conditions that cause similar difficulties. Bring examples of changes in sleep, mood, interest and everyday functioning, including what happens away from work. Depending on the assessment and your preferences, [depression treatment](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/depression-in-adults/treatment/) may involve psychological treatment, medication or other support.

### Use therapy for the difficulties it can address

Therapy may help with depression, anxiety or patterns that keep you overworking, such as [perfectionism at work](/perfectionism-at-work/). It can also help you understand your options when the environment is damaging. It should leave room for the possibility that conditions need to change.

If you are unsure what therapy would involve, the guide to [considering therapy](/considering-therapy/) explains the process. You do not need a final label before having an initial conversation.

### Consider occupational health and practical career support

Where available, occupational health can assess how health and work affect each other and advise on adjustments, leave or a supported return. Career support may help with alternatives if the role cannot be repaired. Decisions about [leaving a job because of anxiety](/my-job-gives-me-anxiety-should-i-quit/) also need to account for income, dependants and realistic options.

## When to get urgent help

If you have thoughts of suicide or self-harm, or your distress is becoming difficult to manage, seek support promptly. You do not need to work out the correct label first.

**Call [999](tel:999) or go to A&E if your life is at risk, you have seriously harmed yourself, or you cannot keep yourself or someone else safe.** The [NHS urgent-help guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/nhs-services/mental-health-services/where-to-get-urgent-help-for-mental-health/) distinguishes these emergencies from urgent support needs.

For urgent mental health support without immediate danger:

- **England:** call [111](tel:111) and select the mental health option, or request an urgent GP appointment.
- **Scotland:** call [111](tel:111) and choose the mental health option through [NHS 24](https://www.nhs24.scot/mental-health-services-at-nhs-24/).
- **Wales:** call [111](tel:111) and press option 2 for [NHS 111 Wales mental health support](https://www.gov.wales/nhs-111-press-2).
- **Northern Ireland:** call [0808 808 8000](tel:08088088000) for [Lifeline crisis support](https://www.publichealth.hscni.net/news/unless-i-called-lifeline-i-would-never-have-known-there-were-solutions).

Across the UK and Ireland, [Samaritans](https://www.samaritans.org/how-we-can-help/contact-samaritan/talk-us-phone/) provides free listening support, day or night, on [116 123](tel:116123). For immediate danger, use emergency services.

## Frequently asked questions

### Am I stressed or burned out if I can still do my job?

Continuing to work does not settle the distinction. Look at what maintaining your output requires: extra hours, repeated checking, lost sleep or withdrawing from life outside work. A recent increase in demands may explain stress; persistent exhaustion and a changed relationship with work may suggest burnout deserves consideration. These observations are useful to discuss with a professional, especially if the changes persist or your health is deteriorating.

### Is burnout depression, or can burnout cause depression?

WHO gives burnout an occupational framing, while depression is a clinical condition. There is substantial symptom overlap and continuing research disagreement about how separate they are. Burnout and depressive symptoms are associated, but that does not establish a simple progression in every person. Work pressure may contribute to depression, and calling your experience burnout should not prevent assessment for depression when the symptoms warrant it.

### Does feeling better during time off mean I do not have depression?

No. Improvement is useful information about your circumstances, but symptoms can fluctuate and depression can have work-related contributors. Tell a GP or therapist what improved, what remained difficult and what happened on returning. Also explain whether the leave genuinely removed demands. Continuing to monitor messages or expecting a backlog makes the experience different from time away with reliable cover. Neither version provides a diagnosis on its own.

### Can therapy treat burnout, or do I need a different job?

Therapy can help with distress, associated mental health difficulties and personal patterns that contribute to overwork. Recovery may also require changes in workload, management, control or responsibilities. Whether that means adjustments, leave, a transfer or another job depends on the situation and your constraints. A GP or occupational health professional may be useful alongside therapy. You can explore these options without committing immediately to resignation.
