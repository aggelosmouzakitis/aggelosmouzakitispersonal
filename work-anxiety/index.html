Work Anxiety: Why Work Gets Into Your Head and What to Do About It

Work anxiety can come from the job, from patterns that work brings out, or from both. Understanding the sequence helps you decide what needs to change.

You leave a meeting and keep replaying the sentence you wish you had phrased differently. The next morning, you wake up thinking about Slack before you have checked the time. Saturday feels reasonably normal; by Sunday evening, tomorrow’s work has started occupying the room.

Work anxiety means fear, apprehension or worry connected to your job. It can happen before work, during the day or long after you finish. The term describes an experience rather than a separate medical diagnosis, and its severity varies considerably.

Sometimes the workload is impossible or the manager is unpredictable. Sometimes an ordinary request carries an unusually strong fear of getting something wrong. Both can happen together. Before deciding that you need therapy, another job or better boundaries, it helps to look at what happens, what changed and what keeps the anxiety going.

What work anxiety actually looks like

A message arrives asking for a quick conversation. There is no subject line. Before you have any information, you are reviewing recent mistakes and calculating how long your savings would last if you lost the job. In another situation, the anxiety is quieter: a routine email sits unopened because reading it might create a problem you do not feel ready to deal with.

Work anxiety symptoms can affect thinking, behaviour, the body and interactions with other people. The NHS describes anxiety symptoms across these areas, including difficulty concentrating, sleep problems, physical discomfort and avoidance. At work, those symptoms can take forms that initially look like diligence.

  • Thinking: replaying a meeting, assuming a colleague’s short reply means they are annoyed, or treating one mistake as evidence that your whole performance is in question.
  • Behaviour: postponing a task you fear, repeatedly rewriting a straightforward message, checking completed work beyond the agreed standard, or asking whether everything is okay after you have already received an answer.
  • Physical symptoms: feeling sick before logging on, a tense jaw during calls, a racing heart before a presentation, or lying awake thinking about the next day.
  • Interactions: becoming impatient with colleagues, withholding a question because it might expose a gap in your knowledge, or finding it hard to delegate because somebody else’s mistake would feel like your responsibility.

None of those behaviours establishes a diagnosis by itself. Preparing carefully can be appropriate. Checking a calculation can be essential. The question is whether the behaviour serves the task, how much time it takes and whether you can stop once it has served its purpose.

There is a difference between asking which deliverable takes priority and asking your manager for the fourth time whether they are disappointed in you. The first request obtains missing information. The second may briefly settle anxiety without giving you anything new. That distinction becomes useful when looking at anxiety and overthinking.

Work anxiety vs ordinary work stress

A presentation is due on Friday, the material is unfinished and the client has changed the brief. Feeling pressure makes sense. If the pressure eases after the presentation and you return to your usual sleep and behaviour, that looks different from a pattern in which every completed task is immediately replaced by another feared mistake.

Stress usually describes the response to demands or pressures. Anxiety involves fear or apprehension about a possible threat, including something that has not happened. They overlap. Anxiety can be brief and tied to a specific event; stress can continue for months when the demands continue. The neat distinction that stress always ends with the deadline while anxiety has no external cause is misleading.

Pay attention to recovery and changes in behaviour. Can you settle when the demand passes? Are you still able to eat dinner without checking messages? Have ordinary meetings started requiring hours of preparation? Persistent worry, expanding avoidance or disrupted sleep deserves attention, even if you are still meeting your targets.

The workplace also matters when judging whether a response is proportionate. A vague calendar invitation means something different after months of surprise disciplinary meetings than it does in a team where it usually means a routine question. Understanding that history is more useful than labelling every anxious prediction irrational.

Why work makes some people anxious

Work puts income, competence and other people’s expectations in the same place. A mistake can have consequences, and some jobs offer very little control over avoiding those consequences. The World Health Organization identifies excessive workloads, low control, unclear roles, limited support and job insecurity among risks to mental health at work.

Research supports taking those conditions seriously. A systematic meta-review of work-related mental health risks found evidence concerning high demands, low control, insecurity, bullying and other adverse conditions. The quality of the underlying reviews varied, and population findings cannot identify the cause of one person’s anxiety. They do make it unreasonable to assume that distress at work is automatically a problem with the employee’s mindset.

Conditions that need attention at work

An unreasonable workload can leave somebody permanently behind, despite competent work. If the team has lost two people and their responsibilities have been redistributed without anything being removed, there may be no way to finish within the available hours. More efficient planning cannot resolve every staffing problem.

Unclear expectations create a different difficulty. A manager asks for more ownership but does not explain which decisions you can make. Priorities change without acknowledgement. Work is judged against a standard that becomes visible only after you have missed it. Anxiety can develop around trying to anticipate rules that nobody has stated.

A difficult manager or team can make ordinary interactions threatening. Public criticism, exclusion, harassment or unpredictable reactions need to be considered as working conditions. So does job insecurity: restructuring, a declining client pipeline or an expiring contract gives people a reason to worry about income. Psychological support can help with the effects while practical action addresses the exposure.

New responsibility and insufficient support

A promotion may bring responsibility for people or decisions you have not handled before. The company treats the title change as proof that you are ready, while you are still learning how to give difficult feedback or judge an unfamiliar risk. Training, clearer authority and access to someone experienced may be necessary.

There can also be a genuine mismatch between a role and the work someone wants to do. Enjoying technical work does not mean enjoying management. Becoming anxious in a new role deserves investigation before it is interpreted as resistance to growth. Questions about career change and professional identity sometimes become relevant here, alongside the practical question of role fit.

Standards, exposure and self-worth

An internal update takes an hour to prepare, then another hour to check. The information was adequate after the first pass, but you keep working because a possible objection feels difficult to tolerate. For some people, perfectionism develops around preventing criticism or embarrassment. Others learned the habit in a workplace that penalised small mistakes.

A 2024 meta-analysis of perfectionism and psychological symptoms found that concerns about mistakes and others’ expectations had stronger associations with distress than striving for high standards. Those associations do not establish a single cause. They support looking more closely at what happens when a standard is missed, rather than assuming that ambition itself is the problem.

Fear of being exposed as inadequate can make learning feel dangerous. A promotion creates more visibility; instead of asking for the information you need, you try to make sure nobody notices you need it. The resulting preparation may improve the work while making each meeting harder to face.

Work can also become central to self-worth. A disappointing review has implications for pay and promotion. If it additionally becomes evidence that you are a disappointing person, the emotional consequences extend well beyond the review. The relationship between achievement, perfectionism and self-worth is worth examining when the same conclusion follows very different professional setbacks.

Anxiety arriving from elsewhere

Work related anxiety can intensify during a breakup, illness, financial difficulty or a period of poor sleep. Someone with an existing anxiety disorder may find that work becomes the main focus of their worry. Someone without a diagnosis can also become more anxious at work while struggling elsewhere.

First-person accounts show how mixed the explanations can be. In a Mental Health Forum discussion about work anxiety, one contributor described supportive colleagues alongside fear of being judged in a new management role; another connected work self-doubt to a marriage breakdown. These are individual accounts, not evidence of how common either pattern is. They illustrate why the timeline needs to include life outside the office.

Is work causing the anxiety, or is anxiety attaching itself to work?

Start with when the pattern changed. Consider what happened before the anxiety became persistent: a manager changed, a promotion arrived, sleep deteriorated, a relationship ended or the company announced cuts. Then look at what reliably intensifies it and what makes a difference.

Being anxious across several jobs does not prove that the problem is entirely personal. Similar working conditions can recur across an industry. A supportive manager does not cancel out understaffing, discrimination or a role that is unsuitable. Equally, a difficult job does not explain every behaviour somebody develops around it.

Relief on holiday is useful information, but it does not settle the cause. Time away removes both workplace demands and opportunities for checking or feared evaluation. Persistent anxiety on holiday also fails to prove that work is innocent: concern about returning, unresolved responsibilities and the effects of previous treatment can continue while you are away.

If it is manageable, keep a brief record for a week or two. Note the event, what you feared would happen, what you did next and whether that response helped beyond the next few minutes. Include an entry when something goes better than expected. A record containing only bad moments can distort the picture.

Use that information to choose a change you can observe. If unclear priorities are the recurring trigger, ask for an explicit order and see what happens. If priorities are already clear but you keep seeking confirmation that you have not upset anyone, work on that sequence too. You do not need to calculate what percentage is the workplace’s fault before taking either problem seriously.

Why work anxiety follows you home

An unresolved task can keep returning to mind because there is a decision still to make or no clear point at which you will resume it. A 2026 meta-analysis of unfinished work tasks found associations with work-related thoughts outside working hours, particularly emotionally charged rumination. This supports taking unfinished work seriously; it does not mean the brain is incapable of resting until the entire task list is complete.

Rumination is repetitive thinking that keeps returning to the same problem without producing much useful movement. Replaying a meeting may initially help identify something to clarify. After the tenth replay, you may still have no new information, while your evening has become a continuation of the meeting.

Checking can keep that process going. Opening Slack tells you whether something has happened, but it may also bring a new request into an otherwise quiet evening. If nothing has happened, the relief can be brief. Ten minutes later, the possibility that something has changed gives you another reason to check.

The pattern is particularly difficult when responsibility has no agreed boundary. A founder may genuinely have decisions that cannot wait. A manager may be covering an operational problem. Yet responsibility for a result does not, by itself, explain which situations require evening availability. Without an agreed distinction, every possible problem can start to feel like yours to catch.

Research on psychological detachment from work links being able to disengage with better sleep and less exhaustion. Much of that evidence is correlational, and both job conditions and personal patterns are involved. Having trouble switching off should prompt examination of the arrangement, rather than another demand to become better at relaxing.

Why Sunday evening can be worse

For a Monday-to-Friday worker, Sunday brings the next working day close enough to anticipate. A difficult meeting, unfinished task or return to a hostile environment may begin occupying attention before anything has happened. The same pattern can occur before a shift or after annual leave; the calendar day is incidental.

Checking the inbox may feel like preparation. Sometimes it is. If it produces another hour of imagined conversations without changing Monday’s plan, it has also extended work into the weekend. Notice whether a short preparation period leaves you able to stop, or becomes an attempt to remove every uncertainty about the week.

What work anxiety can do to performance and relationships

Anxiety can make it harder to concentrate because attention keeps returning to a possible mistake or somebody else’s reaction. Avoidance can delay a conversation until the problem is harder to resolve. Repeated reassurance-seeking may put strain on a colleague who cannot provide the certainty being requested. These are possible consequences, not an inevitable decline in competence.

Some people continue producing excellent work. They compensate with more hours, more checking and less rest. Their performance review may remain positive while getting through the day becomes increasingly expensive in sleep and attention. Good performance does not establish that the current way of working is sustainable.

At home, the effect can be ordinary and cumulative. A partner asks about dinner while you are mentally composing tomorrow’s email. A small request gets a sharper answer than it deserves. You spend the evening asking what your manager meant and have little attention left for what your partner is trying to say.

When work is affecting a relationship, acknowledging the pressure is only part of the conversation. The practical questions include how evenings are being used, what interruptions are genuinely necessary and what each person can expect. A partner can offer support without becoming responsible for settling every work-related fear.

What can actually help

Choose an intervention that fits the problem you have identified. A relaxation exercise may help you get through a difficult moment. It will leave an impossible workload unchanged. A better workload may reduce distress while leaving a checking habit that now happens automatically. Both observations matter when judging whether a change has helped.

Changes to the work itself

The HSE’s Management Standards examine demands, control, support, relationships, role and organisational change. They offer more specific places to look than asking whether the workplace is “toxic”. A friendly company can still assign unmanageable work; an imperfect company may be able to improve the particular conditions causing difficulty.

Translate the problem into something that can be discussed and checked:

  • For competing deadlines: “I can complete the report or the launch analysis by Friday within the available hours. Which takes priority, and what moves?”
  • For a vague standard: “Before I start, can we agree what this needs to include, who approves it and what would count as finished?”
  • For missing feedback: “Which parts of my work are meeting expectations, and what specifically needs to change?”
  • For evening availability: “Outside my agreed hours, which situations need a response? Can we use a separate route for those so ordinary messages can wait?”

Follow a useful conversation with a brief written record of the agreement. If priorities still conflict, that is information about the working arrangement. An instruction to improve your time management is inadequate when the agreed work exceeds the available time.

The advice changes when speaking up carries a credible risk. If a manager retaliates, disclosure or direct confrontation may be unsafe for your employment. Depending on the situation, a trusted senior colleague, union representative, occupational health service or employment adviser may be a better first contact. HR may have a role, but it should not be assumed to offer the same confidentiality as a clinician.

Behavioural changes

At the end of the day, identify the most pressing unfinished item and record the next action and when you will take it. “Sort out the project” leaves a large, undefined demand. “At 9:30 tomorrow, ask Sam to confirm which figures the client needs” gives you a point of return.

A study of end-of-day planning found that making plans for incomplete goals improved detachment among employees prone to difficulty switching off. It was one study, with a sample of 103 employees, rather than proof of a universal solution. A credible plan can help; a plan containing more work than the day allows needs renegotiation.

Agree notification and response arrangements before making changes where your role requires availability. An on-call worker needs a reliable emergency route. Someone checking solely to see whether a routine message has arrived may be able to remove notifications, put the phone elsewhere during dinner or try a bounded period without checking. A walk or change of room after closing the laptop can help mark the transition, but it should not become another ritual you must perform perfectly.

For repeated reassurance, start by noticing the second request for the same answer. Delay it for a manageable period and observe what happens to the urge. The Centre for Clinical Interventions explains how frequent reassurance can bring short relief while maintaining anxiety. Applying this to work requires judgement: asking for missing instructions remains appropriate, and new evidence may warrant another conversation.

The NHS suggests setting aside a short period to address worries. Write down the concern, distinguish a problem you can act on from a hypothetical outcome, and schedule any action. If the concern returns, refer back to the plan rather than automatically reopening the investigation. This is something to try, not a requirement to stop having thoughts.

Pay attention to sleep and the ordinary activities that disappear when work takes over. The NHS guidance on work-related stress includes breaks, movement and changes to work arrangements. Eating regularly and seeing someone after work will not make an abusive manager reasonable, but neglecting those needs can leave you with less capacity to respond. If a technique becomes another task you are failing, simplify it.

Psychological work

Take a specific recent episode. Your manager asked for an amendment, you concluded they regretted hiring you, and you worked until midnight. There are separate questions here: what the amendment required, what evidence supports the conclusion about your manager, and what the extra hours were intended to prevent.

Examine the feared consequence rather than jumping straight to positive thinking. If your performance is genuinely under review, obtain clear expectations and appropriate advice. If the conclusion rests largely on the tone of one message, compare it with the rest of the available evidence. A reasonable assessment can still contain risk; it does not need to guarantee that everything will be fine.

Some behaviours used to reduce anxiety also prevent you from discovering whether the feared outcome would happen without them. If every ordinary email gets six checks, a satisfactory response may be attributed to those checks. The belief that fewer checks would be dangerous remains untested.

On a suitable, low-stakes task, a behavioural experiment might involve predicting what will happen after one appropriate review, sending the message and comparing the result with the prediction. Preserve required quality and safety checks. This approach is for unnecessary precautions around manageable risks; it is inappropriate for testing whether bullying or a dangerous working condition can be tolerated.

Psychological work may also examine what a professional setback has come to mean. Losing a role can threaten income and plans. Feeling that it would also erase your standing as a person is an additional problem worth working on. The purpose is to make professional outcomes easier to respond to without requiring every outcome to go well.

Does work anxiety mean you should quit?

No. Work anxiety is a reason to investigate the situation, and leaving may be a sensible result of that investigation. Persistent mistreatment, unmanageable demands or a role that cannot be made workable deserve serious consideration, alongside income and health. Therapy does not create an obligation to stay.

If a recurring anxiety pattern is involved, changing jobs may help without resolving all of it. That is a reason to address the pattern alongside the decision, rather than proof that a move would be pointless. For the fuller decision, see the guide to deciding whether to quit your job.

When professional help makes sense

Seek support when anxiety is repeatedly disrupting sleep, making it hard to attend or complete work, causing panic, narrowing what you can do or affecting life at home. You can also seek help before reaching that point. There is no need to demonstrate that the job has become unbearable or to exhaust every self-help suggestion first.

A GP or another qualified medical professional is a useful starting point when symptoms are severe, new or physically concerning. They can assess possible medical contributors, consider an anxiety disorder or another condition and discuss treatment. The NHS explains how a GP assesses generalised anxiety disorder, including the range and duration of worries and their effect on daily life. If you cannot currently work, assessment and a discussion about leave or changes to duties may be more useful than trying to force yourself through another week.

For generalised anxiety disorder, NICE recommends treatment according to severity and impact, including guided self-help, cognitive behavioural therapy, applied relaxation or medication where appropriate. That guidance concerns a diagnosed condition. It does not mean everyone with anxiety about work needs the same treatment.

In individual psychotherapy, useful work can begin with a concrete incident and follow what happened afterwards. It might involve testing predictions, reducing repetitive checking, understanding why feedback carries so much personal weight or planning a difficult conversation. The therapist should remain willing to recognise a workplace problem and review whether the work is helping.

If the main need is information about a career move, a practical plan or employment advice, another professional may fit better. The distinction between therapy and coaching depends on what needs attention, rather than how senior or functional you appear. Before starting therapy, ask how the practitioner would work with your situation and how progress would be assessed.

Medical safety: Do not assume new chest pain or severe breathing difficulty is anxiety. NHS guidance on chest pain identifies situations requiring immediate medical help, including persistent sudden chest pain or chest pain with breathlessness. If you cannot keep yourself safe, seek urgent mental health help or contact emergency services where you are. An article or a routine consultation is insufficient for an emergency.

Frequently asked questions

Why do I get anxiety about going to work every day?

Daily anxiety can reflect repeated exposure to a difficult workplace, anticipation of a particular task or a broader anxiety pattern. Notice what you expect to happen before you arrive, and what happens once the day starts. Feeling better after arriving may suggest that anticipation contributes, but it does not establish the whole explanation. A persistent daily pattern deserves attention even when you manage to get through the work.

Why is Sunday evening worse when I felt fine on Saturday?

The return to work is nearer, so unresolved tasks and feared interactions can become more immediate. For some people, the distress also concerns losing control over their time or returning to work they no longer want. Identify the part of Monday you are anticipating. If the feeling starts before every shift rather than on Sundays, the same investigation applies. The timing alone cannot tell you whether changing jobs is necessary.

Can anxiety make it difficult or impossible to work?

Yes. Anxiety can interfere with concentration, attendance, decisions and interactions sufficiently to make working difficult. If you cannot work due to anxiety, arrange a clinical assessment rather than relying on an online symptom checklist. What happens next depends on severity, the job and available support. Treatment, temporary leave or changes to duties may be considered. The NHS discusses support for returning to work after mental health difficulties, including a gradual return where appropriate.

Is work anxiety the same as burnout?

No. Work anxiety describes fear or worry related to work. The WHO describes burnout as an occupational phenomenon involving exhaustion, distance or cynicism towards work and reduced professional efficacy. They can overlap, and sleep or concentration problems do not distinguish them reliably. Depression and physical conditions can also affect energy. Assessment is useful when symptoms persist, particularly if exhaustion or loss of interest extends beyond work.

Should I tell my manager I have anxiety?

Consider what you need to request, whether your manager is trustworthy and who will receive the information. You may be able to start with a concrete discussion about workload or expectations. Health-related support can require further disclosure, depending on the arrangement. Mind’s guidance on telling an employer discusses the decision in a UK context; legal rights vary by country. Ask how information will be handled before sharing sensitive details.

Can therapy help if my workplace is supportive?

Yes. A supportive workplace can coexist with fear of evaluation, persistent checking or difficulty tolerating uncertainty. Therapy can investigate those patterns while checking for practical pressures that have been missed. It can also help when anxiety continues after earlier workplace mistreatment. Reading about what to expect from therapy can help you assess the process. Ask about confidentiality and its limits before beginning.
